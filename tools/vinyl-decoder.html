Vinyl Matrix & Runout Code Decoder

Last verified: 6 August 2026

Before you start

The runout — the blank ring between the last groove and the label, sometimes called the “dead wax” — often has a hand-etched or machine-stamped code in it. Type what you can actually read below and this tool checks it against a documented set of mastering-engineer initials, pressing-plant codes, and formatting conventions, and gives you ranked, honestly-uncertain matches. It won’t pretend to know things nobody has written down.

Scope: this covers commonly-documented codes — mostly US and UK, spanning several eras from the mid-20th century to modern reissues. Runout conventions are crowd-sourced, regional, and genuinely full of gaps even in the best specialist references. If your code doesn’t match anything, that means it isn’t in our documented set yet — not that it’s fake or meaningless.

Reading the disc

Before you type it in — how to actually read a worn runout

Getting the characters off the disc is usually the hard part, decoding them is the easy part. Collectors lean on a few low-tech tricks: a jeweler’s loupe or a simple magnifying glass held under a light angled across the surface (not straight down) makes shallow etching cast a shadow and pop out far more than it does to the naked eye. This matters enough that people specifically ask for magnifier recommendations when their eyesight makes reading runouts hard — it’s a genuine bottleneck before you ever get to decoding.

Cross-checking

The runout is one clue, not the only one

It’s tempting to treat the runout as the single source of truth, but experienced collectors cross-check it against the label, the jacket printing, and the barcode before drawing conclusions — because any one of those can be swapped or misprinted on its own. A documented real case: a copy of Megadeth’s Peace Sells… But Who’s Buying? had a runout matching a 1991 Greek pressing, but the jacket was printed for a Holland release, carried a barcode belonging to a different 1986 Holland pressing, and had the artist’s name misspelled on the label. No single clue proved anything on its own — it was the combination of mismatches across runout, jacket, barcode, and label together that exposed the copy as a counterfeit.

The practical takeaway: if a match here doesn’t line up with what’s printed on the sleeve or the barcode, that mismatch is worth chasing before you trust either one in isolation.

Open questions

Why some runouts look scratched-out or altered

It’s a common thing to see — a number or mark that looks partially crossed out or overwritten — and there isn’t one universal explanation. Two separate, documented ones exist:

  • The plant sometimes replaced a worn pressing master with a fresh one cut from the same mother, and the old etching stayed partly visible underneath the new marks.
  • Other times it’s an internal metalworking tracking mark scratched directly onto the physical stamper tool itself — never meant to be read or interpreted by the person who eventually buys the record.

In our own research we came across a case where only one side of a disc carried this kind of mark and the other didn’t, and nobody in the discussion could pin down why. Treat an unexplained scratch-out as a genuinely open question rather than something this tool (or anyone else) can always resolve from the text alone.

The pressing chain

From lacquer to stamper — why some copies genuinely sound better

Every vinyl pressing traces back through a short chain of physical parts, and understanding it explains why collectors chase specific stampers rather than just a catalog number:

  1. Lacquer (master) — the original cut. One lacquer can produce roughly 2–10 mothers (sources disagree on the exact range, some say as few as 2–4 — it varies by era and plant).
  2. Mother — a positive copy used to make stampers, not to press records directly. Each mother can typically produce another 4–10 stampers.
  3. Stamper — the actual negative that presses records. Each one wears down after roughly 1,000 pressings or so.

A numerically “later” stamper isn’t inherently worse — it’s just a different physical part from the same lineage. What actually matters for sound quality is wear: a heavily-worn stamper, whatever its number, presses audibly softer, duller-sounding copies than a fresh one. That’s the real reason stamper numbers matter to collectors, not the number itself.

Myth

Myth: a low matrix number means an early pressing

This trips up a lot of people new to runout-reading, and it got corrected directly in a vinyl collecting community thread we came across in our research. The matrix number is tied to the mastering and the catalog number, not to any individual disc — so it is normally identical across every copy of that release, whether it was pressed on day one or five years later. A “low” matrix number tells you nothing about press order by itself.

What can vary copy to copy is the stamper number, and even that correlation with press order isn’t reliable — some releases didn’t use individually numbered stampers at all. Thriller is a cited example where they weren’t used. If you want to estimate how early a copy is, the stamper (and its wear) is a far better signal than the matrix number — and even then, treat it as a lead, not proof.

Myth

Myth: side A and side B matrix numbers have to match

Another reasonable-sounding assumption that doesn’t hold up: that both sides of a record should show matching or obviously-related plate/matrix generations, and that a mismatch signals something wrong with the copy. Collectors have documented real counter-examples — a Cream Disraeli Gears pressing among them — where the two sides genuinely came from different plates or generations. A post claiming the sides “have to match” was made in a vinyl collecting community and got downvoted for it. Don’t assume a mismatch between sides means one side is fake or wrong.

Honest limits

What this tool can and can’t do

What it does: scores your transcribed runout text (partial and worn input included) against a documented set of mastering-engineer marks, pressing-plant codes, and formatting conventions, and gives you ranked candidates with an honest confidence level and any known caveats.

What it doesn’t do:

  • It isn’t a Discogs lookup or a full pressing database — it's a plain-language decoder for the conventions themselves.
  • It can’t confirm anything with certainty. Even a “well-documented” match is a documented convention, not a guarantee about your specific disc.
  • It can’t authenticate a pressing or catch a counterfeit by itself — cross-check the jacket, barcode, and label too.
  • It doesn’t cover every regional or era-specific convention that exists. This space has real, acknowledged gaps.
